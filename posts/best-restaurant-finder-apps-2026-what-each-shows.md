You have already searched. Four apps are open, and they all agree there is a
restaurant two hundred metres away. The problem was never finding a
restaurant — it is that the four results answer four different questions, and
the one you actually need is sitting in the one you are not looking at.

A friend's friend rated it. The map says it closes in twenty minutes. The
other app has a photo of the dish you came here for. And the fourth one has no
photo at all, just a line that says cash only, which is the only fact you
could not have learned from a review.

**These tools split into two camps: the ones that tell you what people thought
of a place, and the ones that tell you what a place actually is right now.**
You need both, and you need to know which question you are asking before you
scroll.

## What the tools actually do

Google Maps and Apple Maps are the two defaults. Both find places near you and
show hours, ratings, photos and a walking route. Google is the deeper of the
two for restaurant detail — its review base is the largest, and it shows
"open now" hours and a photo stream of what the food and the room look like.
Apple Maps is lighter on restaurant specifics in many regions, but it runs on
the same streets and pins, and on Apple phones it is the one you already reach
for when you are already walking.

Yelp and TripAdvisor are the review platforms. Both are free, both lean on
user reviews and photos, and both let a business maintain its own listing —
cuisine, price range, and a set of amenity tags. If you are choosing between
ten places you have never heard of, a review with a name on it is usually more
useful than any tag, because it describes what actually happened at the table.

OpenStreetMap sits underneath all of this. It is the free, crowd-mapped
database of the world's places, and it is what several of the map apps lean on
where their own data is thin — Apple has used it in some regions since iOS 6.
Its value is structural: it tags places with structured facts — what they
serve, whether they take a card, whether there is outdoor seating — rather
than opinions. It is excellent in dense cities and patchy everywhere else, and
it goes out of date, because it depends on people keeping it current.

## What reviews tell you — and what they don't

Reviews answer one question well: what was it like to eat there? A long,
specific review from someone who describes the room and the food is worth
more than a score of any size. Why a 4.4 abroad is not the same 4.4 at home
is covered by the [post on review scores](/blog/why-review-scores-are-a-bad-way-to-choose-a-restaurant-when-travelling/).

But the question that decides most of your evenings is not what it was like.
It is: **is it open now, will I be able to eat what I need to eat, and can I
get through the door?** Reviews rarely answer those, and the places that do —
the hours, the dietary tags, the accessibility notes — are self-reported and
go stale quietly. A place that changed its hours last month still shows the
old ones. A kitchen that added a vegan line does not flag it, and one that
stopped does not remove it. A "wheelchair accessible" tag is whatever the
person who entered it decided it meant.

None of this is a reason to stop using the tool you already have. It is a
reason to know which of the two jobs it is doing, and which one it is not
doing. The [post on what "open now" actually means](/blog/what-open-now-actually-means/)
is the one worth keeping on hand, because the hours problem is the one that
costs the most wasted evenings.

## The details that decide the visit

The details that actually send you a different way fall into four buckets,
and they all share the same weakness: they are claims, not checks.

- **Open right now.** Hours on every major app are self-reported. The gap
  between the listed close and the kitchen's real close is where a wasted
  evening is made. The data problem behind hours — kitchen close, split hours,
  public holidays — is the reason a list of "open" places can be wrong in the
  one place you are standing.
- **Diet options.** Vegan, vegetarian, halal, kosher, gluten-free,
  dairy-free. On the review platforms these are self-declared by the business.
  They tell you the place wants to serve you; they do not tell you the kitchen
  behind the pass is built for it. If you have coeliac disease or a severe
  allergy, this is where checking stops helping and asking begins —
  cross-contamination is real and only the venue can speak to their own
  kitchen, which is the whole argument of the posts on eating gluten-free and
  on severe allergies abroad.
- **Access.** A wheelchair can be stopped at the step or at the table, and
  the toilet is the one nobody checks. A tag tells you the place declared
  itself accessible; it does not tell you where the step is. Accessibility
  means different things in different countries, so describe what to verify
  rather than trusting what you are shown.
- **Payment.** The one fact a review never gives you and a directory can:
  whether the till will take the card you pulled out.

## Which tool for which question

Pick by the question, not by habit.

- You are vetting whether a place is worth the trip. Reviews and ratings win.
  Read the specific ones, weigh the recent ones, and treat the number as a
  baseline you do not know.
- You need to know if it is open tonight and how to get there. Open a map —
  Google or Apple, whichever is on the phone — and check the hours before you
  walk. This is the check that saves the most wasted evenings for the least
  effort.
- The decision hinges on a diet option, a step, or a card. Look for a place
  that declares the specific thing you need, then confirm by phone. Walking
  down the street and reading the menu still works, and asking a local still
  works; checking first is what saves the hour.
- You want the facts rather than the opinions, in one list, merged from more
  than one map. There are a small number of directory-style tools built on
  open data that show a place's details once it is open and how long the walk
  really is, without a rating. NearEats is one of them, an iPhone app built
  from OpenStreetMap and Apple Maps so a venue found in both shows once with
  the best detail from each, and it is a $1.99 paid app rather than a free
  ad-supported one — a deliberate trade, since it carries no ratings, no
  reviews and no user photos, because no open source provides those honestly.
  It shows whether a place is open right now, its diet options, wheelchair
  access and accessible toilets, and red badges for the two things that ruin a
  visit: cash only, and not wheelchair accessible.

The honest version of the comparison is not that one of these is the best. It
is that they are answering different questions, and the review tools — the
ones with the big names and the free price — are the ones most likely to leave
you at the closed door.

## The short version

- Reviews answer "what was it like". Use them to vet, not to time.
- A map answers "is it open and how do I get there". Check the hours before
  you walk.
- A dietary or access tag answers "does it want to serve me". Confirm the
  kitchen with the place, especially when a wrong guess costs days.
- A directory answers "what is it, right now, from the open data". Use it
  when the decision is a fact, not a taste.
- The answer to "which app" changes with the question. Five minutes of
  checking the one detail you cannot guess is worth the wasted evening it
  saves.
