You have landed, you are hungry, and the menu has one green symbol next to it:
a side salad, or maybe fries. That is not a meal, it is a garnish. The question
is not whether the place has anything vegan — it has, the salad. The question
is whether the kitchen can build you a full main course without animal
products, and whether you can tell from a distance rather than after a
twenty-minute walk to find out the hard way.

The good news is that this is a test you can run before you arrive. A salad on
the menu tells you very little. What matters is whether the kitchen is the kind
of place that can feed you, and most of the signals are visible ahead of time.

## Start with the cuisine, not the menu's vegan symbol

Before you read a single menu line, look at what the place *is*. Some food
traditions are built around plant-based cooking, and in those you will usually
find a real main without asking: many Indian and Ethiopian kitchens, the
vegetarian traditions of parts of East and Southeast Asia, meze-style
Mediterranean plates, and the curry and dhal counters you see across the world.
If the restaurant's core is a protein centrepiece — a steakhouse, a grill,
classic European fine dining — your options will usually be an afterthought, or
a salad.

Treat that as a prior, not a promise. A vegetarian tradition is not
automatically vegan: ghee, dairy and eggs appear in plenty of dishes that would
otherwise be fine, and honey shows up in sweet ones. And vegan options vary
hugely by region, so nothing here is a rule for a country — it is a starting
guess about the kitchen in front of you.

## The main-course test

Now read the menu for the one thing that matters: a dish that is plant-based by
design, not a meat dish with the meat removed.

- A hot dish with a starch, a protein and something cooked — a dhal, a bean or
  lentil dish, tofu or tempeh, seitan, a grain-and-nut bowl. This is the signal
  you want.
- A dish you have to assemble from a list of sides — rice, a salad, a dip.
  That is the "has a salad" answer in disguise.
- A "vegetarian" or "veggie" section, read as a starting list. In many
  cuisines it still contains dairy and egg, so shortlist the dishes and ask.

A dish that is plant-based by design is a good sign the kitchen can feed you,
but it is only a shortlist: a dish without meat can still be made with meat
stock, butter or ghee, or egg, so confirm those next (see below). And if the
only vegan thing is a bare set of sides you assemble yourself, the kitchen
usually is not built to feed you.

## Where animal products hide in plant-looking dishes

This is where vegan trips quietly fail, because the dish *looks* plant-based and
the animal product is in the cooking, not on top of it. These are the ones worth
asking about, and the real reason for each:

- **Broth and stock.** Vegetable soups and risottos are often made with meat,
  chicken or fish stock. The risk is the liquid, not the vegetables.
- **Butter and ghee.** Ghee is clarified butter, and it is used to finish or fry
  vegetables across many cuisines. Cream does the same job in soups and curries.
- **Eggs.** Fresh pasta, some noodles, and some batters and dumplings take egg.
  Dried pasta in many places does not, so the type of pasta changes the answer.
- **Fish sauce, shrimp paste, oyster sauce, and dashi.** These season a lot of
  East and Southeast Asian cooking. Dashi is a stock traditionally made with
  kombu and bonito, so a "vegetable" miso soup or noodle broth may still carry
  fish.
- **Cheese.** All cheese is out for a vegan; some hard cheeses are also made
  with animal rennet, which is mainly a concern for strict vegetarians.
- **Honey and lard.** Honey shows up in marinades and sweet dishes, and some
  fried and baked items — certain beans, pastries, some breads — are cooked in
  animal fat. These vary by region and recipe, so ask rather than assume.

You do not need every one of these to be absent; the point is that the answer
lives in the kitchen, not in the dish name. And your own line on honey, on
wine and beer clarified with animal products, and on traces is yours to set —
there is no universal standard for where a vegan draws it.

## What to ask, and how to ask it so the answer is usable

In many places, staff will hear "vegan" and reach for the salad. The question
that gets you a real answer names what you are excluding:

- "What can you make without meat, fish, dairy or eggs?" — not just "is this
  vegan?"
- "What is the broth made from?" — this is the one that settles the soups.
- "Are the vegetables cooked in butter or oil?" — the one that settles the
  vegetable dishes.
- "Could the kitchen make me a full dish from these?" — some kitchens will
  improvise a main when asked; others will not, and you learn which kind it is.

If the menu is not in your language, a short note listing what you do not eat
(meat, fish, dairy, eggs) works better than the single word "vegan", because the
list is harder to misread. Do not assume the word means the same thing
everywhere, or that the staff are trained on it. Asking plainly and
specifically is enough.

## How to check before you walk over

The manual versions of this work and always will: read the menu posted
online, phone ahead, ask a local, or walk past and read the board. What
checking first saves is the twenty minutes of walking to discover the only
vegan option was a salad. In a tourist ring near a station, options are
usually thinner; see [how to avoid the tourist-trap
ring](/blog/how-to-avoid-the-tourist-trap-ring-around-stations-and-cathedrals/).

NearEats is one way to do this check: it shows vegan and vegetarian tags from
its OpenStreetMap and Apple Maps data, and whether the place is open now, before
you set off. Read the tags as an indicator, not a guarantee — they are only as
current as their source data, and OpenStreetMap is crowd-mapped, excellent in
dense cities and patchy elsewhere, and occasionally out of date. The menu and a
phone call still settle it.

## The honest limit

Menus change and kitchens change, and a dish that is vegan today can be cooked a
different way next week. In many places the word "vegan" on a menu has no legal
definition, so a symbol is a claim, not a certification. And in small towns and
rural areas the options can be thin — plan a fallback meal for that evening.

One thing to separate cleanly: this post is for a dietary or ethical choice.
If your restriction is a milk, egg or fish **allergy**, that is a different risk
profile — the dish that fails you here can send you to a hospital — and the
right reading is the [severe food allergy guide](/blog/eating-out-with-a-severe-food-allergy-abroad/), not the
lighter assumptions this post makes.

## The short version

- Look at the kitchen, not the symbol. Plant-based traditions usually have a
  real main; protein-centrepiece kitchens usually do not.
- Run the main-course test: a plant-based dish on its own beats a list of sides
  you assemble yourself.
- Ask about the stock, the fat and the eggs by name — that is where the animal
  product hides.
- List what you exclude; a list is harder to misread than the word "vegan".
- Confirm before you walk over, online or by phone, and treat any tag as an
  indicator, not a guarantee.
- If it is an allergy rather than a choice, read the allergy guide instead.
