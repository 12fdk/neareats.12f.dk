You land in a city, open the map app, and the first thing you do is sort by
rating. You pick the 4.6 over the 4.4. You sit down, the food arrives, and
it's fine — or it's a 4.6 in a city where 4.6 is what a mediocre sandwich
shop gets when the owner's aunt writes eleven five-star reviews in the first
month.

**Review scores are relative to their city, not absolute.** A 4.4 in a city
where restaurants run a tight operation and the median diner is not a food
critic is a very different place from a 4.4 in a city where the baseline is
a two-star experience and the 4.4 is genuinely surprising. You are importing
your expectations from a different market and the number doesn't tell you
that.

This is not an argument against reviews. It's an argument about what the
number is actually telling you, and what to do with the rest of the
information you can actually get before you sit down.

## What the score does and doesn't tell you

A review score, in the best case, tells you where a place sits relative to
other places in the same city, at the same price point, with the same kind
of service. That's genuinely useful if you know the baseline. You don't,
because you've just arrived.

What it doesn't tell you:

- **Whether the place will serve you what you want.** A 4.6 Italian place
  may not have a gluten-free option, may be cash-only, may close the
  kitchen at 9:30 and the room at 10, and the review average won't tell
  you any of that. If the question is gluten specifically, the
  [gluten-free abroad guide](/blog/eating-gluten-free-abroad-what-to-check/)
  covers the checks that actually settle it. Reviews are written by people
  who already had the meal; the decision you need to make is before the
  meal.
- **Whether the experience will match yours.** Reviews from a business
  dinner are not reviews from a family dinner. A solo diner's experience
  of a place is not a group's. A weekday lunch is not a Saturday night.
  The average flattens all of that into a number.
- **Whether the place is still the same place.** Restaurants change chefs,
  change ownership, change the menu. A 4.7 from two years ago is not a
  4.7 from last week.

The score is a weak prior. Treat it as one input among several, not as the
one that decides.

## The signals that survive travel

Some things you can observe before you commit, and they're more
discriminating than a number:

**The walk-in pace.** A place where the room is turning over quickly and
the kitchen is producing at a steady clip is telling you something about
whether the food is going to be hot and made-to-order when you arrive. A
place that's half-empty at dinner peak with a long wait for the food
between courses is telling you something else. Neither is a verdict, but
both are data.

**What the regulars actually order.** If you can sit for ten minutes —
a coffee on the corner, the newspaper at a table you don't mind at —
watch what the people who come back order. That's the menu the place is
actually good at. The menu on the wall is the menu the place wants you to
order.

**Whether the kitchen can do one thing well.** A small menu with six items
where the kitchen has clearly been doing them for a long time is usually a
better bet than a large menu where every dish is an introduction. Special
focus is a real signal; it's also the kind of thing a 4.6 average from a
tourist-heavy place will miss, because the tourists order the safe thing
and the regulars order the good thing.

**The language test.** If the staff can explain the menu in a language you
both understand, that's a place where a dietary requirement or an allergy
question has a chance of landing. If the exchange is a series of nods, you
have a decision to make before you order, and the review score is not
going to help you make it.

## The one question worth asking before you order

Most of the time, the useful thing to do is not to read more reviews — it's
to ask the one question that the review average can't answer for you.

If you have a dietary requirement, the question is the specific one:
*Is this cooked with flour? Is it in the same pan as something
gluten-containing?* Not "do you have gluten-free options" — that gets you
a nod and a salad. The specific question gets you a real answer, or a
honest "we can't tell you, ask the kitchen."

If you're travelling with a group and nobody can decide, the question is
the one that settles it: *What is the one thing you'd actually want to
eat here, and is it on this menu?* If the answer is "I don't know" for
three of the four people, the place isn't the problem — the decision is.

If you just need to know whether the kitchen is still open, the question
is: *What time does the kitchen close, separate from the room?* In many
cities — especially ones that run late bars — the kitchen closes well
before the room does, and the review score will not tell you by how much.

One honest tool for the last of those — checking whether a place is open
right now, what diet options it lists, whether there's a step you'll get
stuck on — is NearEats, which shows open-now status, diet options and
wheelchair access as plain facts rather than a score. It's one way to do
it; walking down the street and reading the door works too, and asking a
local works, and the review score is a starting point for all of it. But
checking first saves the walk.

## The short version

- **A review score is a relative number, not an absolute one.** 4.4 in
  your home city is not 4.4 in the city you just landed in.
- **The score tells you where the place sits, not whether it will serve
  you.** Open hours, dietary options, cash policy, kitchen close — none of
  that is in the average.
- **Watch the room, not the number.** Walk-in pace, what the regulars
  order, whether the kitchen has one thing it does well — these are more
  discriminating than a decimal point.
- **Ask the specific question, not the general one.** "Is this cooked with
  flour" beats "do you have gluten-free options." "What time does the
  kitchen close" beats the review average.
- **The score is a starting point.** It narrows the field. It doesn't
  decide.
