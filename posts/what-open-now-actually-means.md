You have planned the whole evening around a place. You know the neighbourhood
from the photos, you have the address in your notes, you even asked the hotel
which side of the square it is on. And then you stand in front of the door at
21:40 and the sign says the kitchen closed at 21:00 — while the bar, you will
learn, was open for another two hours. The listing said 12–23. The listing was
not lying. It was describing the bar.

This is the most common way a food app fails someone, and it is worth
understanding why, because the fix is not a better app. The fix is knowing
which number the number is, and checking the right one.

## The two clocks: bar close and kitchen close

Every restaurant that serves alcohol and food has two closing times, and the
one on the sign is usually the wrong one for dinner.

- **Bar close** (or front-of-house close) is when the last drink leaves. This
  is the number that ends up in listings, because it is the number on the
  permit, the number on the door, the number the city records.
- **Kitchen close** is when the kitchen stops cooking. This comes first, and
  sometimes well first. A place that lists 12–23 may stop taking dinner
  orders at 21:00 and serve the last plates at 21:15, after which you can sit
  and have wine until midnight.

The gap between the two is where "open now" goes from helpful to
actively misleading. If you are deciding whether to have dinner there, you
need the kitchen number, not the bar number. If you are deciding whether to
have a drink there, you need the bar number. A listing that does not
distinguish the two is usually giving you the bar number, because that is
the number most commonly available.

**When you call ahead, ask for kitchen close specifically.** "When does the
kitchen stop cooking?" is the single most useful question a restaurant can
answer you before you leave, and almost no digital listing will give it to
you. It is also the number that changes most often, because it tracks the
kitchen's staffing, not the licence.

## Split hours and the midday trap

A meaningful number of restaurants run **split hours** — open 12–14:30 and
19–23, closed in between. This is especially common in small towns, in
tourist areas with a lunch-driven economy, and anywhere the lunch service is
the money and the dinner service is a bonus.

The trap is that the split hour is the easiest one to get wrong, because a
listing that only captures one of the two windows will show the place as
closed at 15:00 when it opens again at 19:00. Or it will show it as open at
15:00 because it is "open 12–23" and the midday gap never got recorded.

**If you are planning anything in the middle of the day at a place that does
not look like a 24-hour restaurant, assume there is a gap and verify.** The
phone call takes ninety seconds. If you are trying to do a late lunch and the
place you want closed its kitchen at 14:30, the gap is not a small
inconvenience, it is the whole plan.

## Public holidays: the schedule that does not apply to your day

The listing you are reading was learned from a normal week. Your day is not a
normal week.

Public holidays break the pattern in three different ways, and they break it
in both directions:

1. **Many restaurants close entirely** on the major holidays of their own
   country. This is especially true in small towns and in countries where the
   holiday is a family day. A place that is reliably open on a random
   Wednesday may be dark on the 25th of December, the day of Eid, or
   whichever holiday your specific city treats as a non-working day.
2. **Some places run a shortened or changed service** on the day after a
   public holiday, or on a long weekend.
3. **Tourist areas sometimes run the reverse schedule** and are open only on
   the weekends and holiday weeks when the tourists are there.

The practical rule: **check the local calendar for your specific date, not
just the weekday.** "Thursday" is not the same thing on a Thursday in
September and a Thursday that is a public holiday in April. If the app you
are using has a public-holiday layer, use it; if it does not, the phone call
is the layer.

## The data problem, explained honestly

Most food apps get their hours from one of two sources, and both have a
specific failure mode.

**OpenStreetMap** is crowd-mapped. A volunteer edits the hours, or the place
posts them, or a visitor who noticed the door closed updates it. This is
excellent in dense city centres where a hundred people walk past the place a
day, and patchy everywhere else. A restaurant that changed its schedule six
months ago and has not been walked past by anyone who cares to edit it will
carry the old schedule. A restaurant that closed permanently last winter and
was never deleted will show "open 12–23" indefinitely.

**Google and other provider feeds** work differently — they scrape the web,
scrape their own reviews, and infer from the place's own website or social
pages. This is better at catching a closure, because a closed business stops
posting, and worse at catching a schedule change, because the schedule
change does not announce itself anywhere.

Neither source is authoritative. Both are the best available, and both are
**stale in the places you are most likely to need them** — the small town
where you arrived on a Sunday, the restaurant that changed its hours when
the chef left, the place that is in the middle of a renovation. The honest
framing is: the app gives you the best available starting point. It is not
the source of truth. The restaurant is the source of truth.

**Walking down the street and reading the sign on the door works. Phoning
ahead works. Asking the hotel receptionist works.** The app is a way to do
the check before you leave the room, so you are not standing in front of a
closed door at 21:40 with a group of hungry people and a flight at 09:00.
NearEats shows whether a place is open right now, and it flags the things
that are likely to be wrong — like a place that is cash-only, or not
wheelchair accessible — which is the same category of problem as a schedule
you cannot trust. It is one way to do the check, alongside the phone call
and the sign on the door. If you are trying to plan the whole late evening —
not just whether a place is open, but where food is actually still going
out the door at 22:30 in a city you don't know —
[finding a place open late](/blog/finding-a-place-open-late-in-a-city-you-dont-know/)
is the bigger problem, and this post is the first part of it.

## What to check, in order

If you are planning an evening around a specific restaurant, the checks are
in a specific order, and the order matters:

1. **The app or the listing, first.** This is the fastest check, and it
   catches the gross errors — a place that is closed today, a place that is
   permanently closed, a place that does not actually serve food. If the app
   says it is open, you have saved yourself the cross-town walk. If it says
   it is closed, you have saved yourself the cross-town walk in the other
   direction.
2. **The phone call, if the stakes are high.** A group dinner, a dietary
   accommodation, a last meal before a flight, a place you have driven an
   hour to get to — the phone call is not optional. Ask for kitchen close,
   ask about the specific date if it is a holiday week, ask about the
   specific dietary need if you have one. Ninety seconds.
3. **The sign on the door, when you arrive.** The sign is the source of
   truth for the day. If the sign contradicts the app, the sign wins. If the
   sign contradicts the phone call, the sign wins and you should ask why.

## The short version

- "Open now" usually means the bar is open, not the kitchen. Ask for
  kitchen close when it matters.
- Split hours are the most common data gap. If you are planning midday,
  verify.
- Public holidays break the schedule. Check the specific date, not the
  weekday.
- The app gives you the best available starting point. The restaurant is the
  source of truth. The sign on the door wins.
- For anything where a wrong answer costs you an evening, the phone call is
  ninety seconds and it settles the question the app cannot.
