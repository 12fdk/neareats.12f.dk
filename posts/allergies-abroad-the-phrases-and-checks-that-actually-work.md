You have landed in a city where you cannot read the menu, and the ingredient
that matters most is the one that never appears on it. The peanut in the
fryer oil, the sesame in the dipping bowl, the crust of bread the kitchen uses
to wipe the pan — none of them is on the plate, none of them is on the menu,
and the person taking your order may have no idea they are a problem.

This is not a vocabulary problem. Fluency would help a little, but the
vocabulary is not where it goes wrong — what goes wrong is the *shape of the
question*. "Does this have peanuts?" is the wrong question, because the
honest answer is often "we don't know." The question that gets a real answer
is the one about the kitchen, and it can be asked before you sit down, in
any language, with a card in your hand.

**Most of the useful work happens before the plate: a pre-visit question, a
written card, and the right wording. None of it is a guarantee — and knowing
exactly where the phrases stop working is part of using them well.**

## Why the card beats the translation

You do not need to speak the language. You need three lines in it, written
before you leave, on something you can physically hand across a table.

Why written beats spoken, in every language:

- **It removes the translation step.** Spoken, your allergy passes through
  your accent, the waiter's vocabulary, their memory, the kitchen's
  understanding of your accent — four lossy hops. A card is one hop, and it
  is a hop the person who cooks the dish can read at their own pace.
- **It names the allergen the way the kitchen will recognise it.** "I am
  allergic to nuts" is a problem: in many cuisines "nuts" means something
  different than what you mean — tree nuts versus peanuts, and the two live
  in different parts of the kitchen. Write the specific name: peanut,
  almond, sesame. In a kitchen that grinds its own sauces, the specific name
  is the only name that locates the danger.
- **It states the stakes once, calmly, and in black and white.** "It can be
  life-threatening, not a preference" does more work than a spoken
  explanation delivered while hungry and mid-conversation. The kitchen reads
  it at the pass, not at the table.

The three lines, in order:

1. I have a severe allergy to [specific allergen].
2. It can be life-threatening. It is not a preference.
3. I am ordering [the dish]. It must have no contact with [allergen] — in
   the oil, the sauce, the equipment, the garnish.

Line three is the one people skip, and it is the one that matters most,
because it ties the card to the dish actually being cooked. A generic card
about the kitchen in general is a document; a card about *this plate* is an
instruction.

One caveat on sourcing: get the three lines checked by someone who actually
lives there, not by a translation app alone. Apps are usually close enough
for the simple sentences, but a phrase that reads perfectly can still be
*wrong in meaning* in the local culinary context — "no nuts" in some
languages is understood as "no tree nuts" and the peanut sauce arrives
anyway. A local friend, a hotel front desk, a line in the local-language
section of a forum: any of these is worth two minutes.

## The question that settles the kitchen

The card handles the table. The question that matters more is the one asked
*before* you sit down — by message, by phone, at the door — because at the
table the kitchen has already started cooking and the answer has become a
negotiation.

Do not ask "do you have [allergen]?" Ask the process question:

- **How does your kitchen handle [allergen]?**
- **If I order [dish], how is it kept apart?** Separate equipment, separate
  oil, separate prep space — or the same fryer as everything else?

Watch the shape of the answer, not just the content. "Separate room,
dedicated equipment, oil changed daily" is a *system* — the safety is built
in. "We can try to be careful" is a *promise* — the safety is built out of
that day's discipline. A kitchen with a system describes it without being
pressed. A kitchen that needs pressing will need it, and the pressing itself
is the information. **A kitchen that cannot or will not answer is an
answer**, and it costs nothing to act on: you choose another place.

This is the same test the [severe-allergy
post](/blog/eating-out-with-a-severe-food-allergy-abroad/) walks through in
full, including the epinephrine logistics — this post is about the *words*,
and the words are only useful if they are aimed at the right kitchen.

## The words that mean what you think they mean

This is where the post gets country-specific, and it matters: the
*legal floor* under allergen communication is not the same height
everywhere, and pretending it is will get you hurt in exactly the places
where the floor is low.

- **In the EU and the UK**, restaurants are legally required to be able to
  tell you about the major allergens in a dish (the EU's fourteen, plus the
  UK's). This includes loose food — the deli counter, the salad bar, the
  buffet. It guarantees the *capacity* to answer, not the care, and it does
  not bind takeaways, home cooking or street vendors in the same way, so
  check what actually applies in the country you are in.
- **In the US**, federal law does not impose the same restaurant disclosure
  duty; state and local rules vary, and chain restaurants often publish
  allergen information voluntarily — a chain's published guide is usually
  the most reliable written source you will find there.
- **Most of the rest of the world** — much of Asia, the Middle East, Latin
  America, Africa — has no comparable restaurant duty. The question is a
  courtesy with no legal weight behind it, and the staff may simply not
  know. That is not a reason to stop asking; it is a reason to ask *early*
  and to treat the answer as information, not as a warranty.

Two vocabulary traps, because they bite across many languages:

- **"Vegetarian" does not mean allergen-free.** It means no meat. The dish
  may still carry your allergen in the sauce, the oil, the garnish —
  vegetarian and allergen-free are two different filters, and conflating
  them is how a peanut-free person orders a peanut dish by accident.
- **"Gluten-free" is a claim about one ingredient, not about your
  allergen.** If your allergen is wheat or rye, the words overlap. If it is
  soy, sesame or dairy, a "gluten-free" dish may still contain all of them.
  The [gluten-free
  post](/blog/eating-gluten-free-abroad-what-to-check/) covers that side of
  the map.

**Where a legal floor exists, use it as a starting point for the
conversation — it guarantees the kitchen *can* answer, and a kitchen that
cannot answer where it must is telling you something. Where no floor exists,
the same question is still the right question; only its weight changes.**

## Where the phrases stop working

The honest limit, stated plainly, because it is the part that keeps the rest
of this post useful: **no phrase, no card, no translation, no law and no
pre-visit call turns a shared kitchen into a guarantee.** The words get the
kitchen to pay attention; they do not remove the shared fryer.

If your allergy is anaphylactic, the words are two tools in a plan, not the
plan. The plan also includes: the epinephrine auto-injector in a pocket you
reach for, not in the bag; the nearest emergency department known on day
one; and the willingness to leave when the answer is "we don't know." The
last of those is the one that separates a managed week from a hospital
evening, and it is the one the phrases depend on.

On the practical side of finding the kitchen worth talking to before you
walk over: reading menus down the street works, phoning ahead works, asking
a local works. An app like NearEats is one way to pre-check a venue's diet
options from the pavement before you commit to the walk — the point is only
to put the question earlier than the plate.

## The short version

- The card is three lines, in the local language, named to the specific
  allergen and to the specific dish. Have a local check the wording.
- Ask the *process* question before you sit down: how is the allergen kept
  apart? Listen for a system, not a promise. No answer is an answer.
- Know where the legal floor stands in the country you are in: EU and UK
  give you the capacity to answer; most of the world gives you a courtesy.
  Ask anyway.
- "Vegetarian" is not "allergen-free," and "gluten-free" is not "allergen-
  free" for most allergens. Two different filters.
- The phrases raise the odds. The epinephrine, the hospital and the
  willingness to leave are what cover the rest.
