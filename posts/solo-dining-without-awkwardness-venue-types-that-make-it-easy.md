"Do you go to fancy bars and restaurants alone?" It is the question you ask a
stranger at some point, or the question you are asking yourself right now,
sitting at a table for two while the empty chair looks a little too
deliberate.

The awkwardness you are imagining is real, but it is mostly a problem of seat
and timing, not of character. Plenty of people eat and drink out alone, every
day, in many cities, and most solo dinners that go badly fail for the same few
avoidable reasons: the wrong table, the wrong hour, and expectations set by a
room that was never built for you.

All of that can be checked before you sit down. This is the check, plus the
venue types where solo works from the start.

## Pick the seat before you pick the place

The same restaurant feels like a different place alone, depending on where you
are sitting. A table for two facing the room puts you in the view of everyone
who enters and leaves, with one empty seat beside you. A counter seat along the
wall puts you facing the kitchen, the bar or the street: you have something to
look at, and the rest of the room becomes background.

**The awkwardness of sitting alone is mostly a seating problem, not a
solitude problem: a seat that faces the room feels exposed, a seat that faces
something does not.**

That is why the same venue is fine on a Tuesday at the counter and unbearable
on a Saturday at the corner table. If a place has any seat that works for one,
that is the seat you want — and the seat you ask for when you book.

## Venue types that are built for one person

Some venue types work for one person by design. The ones that come up most,
and the catch on each:

- **Counter seating and open kitchens.** You watch the work happen and there
  is something to look at for the whole meal. The catch: counter seats are
  often first-come, and the good counters fill early in service.
- **The bar of a pub or cocktail bar.** You order at the bar, the staff know
  the room, and a single seat at the bar is the default, not a problem. The
  catch: the food is often limited, and in some places the bar is standing
  only.
- **Communal or shared tables**, as in food halls and market stalls. Strangers
  sitting together is the design, not an accident. The catch: the noise, and
  the pace of the room.
- **Cafes and all-day places.** Laptops, books and single diners are common.
  The easiest seat in many cities for a slow meal on your own.
- **Ramen-style counters and food-hall stalls**, in the places where that
  format exists. One person, one seat, quick order, quick out. The catch: the
  format is not universal, so do not expect it in every city.
- **Hotel bars.** A hotel bar is used to one person with one drink, and that
  is the whole visit. The catch: it is usually not dinner, and the food story
  ends there.

One caveat runs through all of it: how welcome a table for one is, and how
these customs play out, varies by country and by city. The venue types are the
same; the norms are local, so the door is worth a look.

## Making a solo visit work at a fancier place

The fancy-restaurant version of this works too. The difference is that you say
the thing out loud and check it, instead of walking in and hoping:

- **Book for one, and say where you want to sit.** Say 'a table for one, and a
  seat at the bar or counter if you have one.' Many places keep bar and
  counter seats that cannot be reserved in advance — asking is worth it, and
  it costs nothing.
- **Go at the quiet edge of service.** Early in the evening, or midweek, is
  less exposed than Saturday at the height of it. This works at any venue, not
  just the fancy ones.
- **Bring something to do, without apology.** A book, a notebook, a phone. A
  long meal has gaps, and having something to do in them makes a two-hour solo
  dinner feel like an evening out instead of a vigil.
- **Ask about the menu before you commit.** If you want less, ask for a
  shorter menu or the bar menu. If the kitchen runs a tasting menu, ask
  whether they serve it to one person: pairings, minimums and half portions
  are the venue's decision, and the only way to know is to ask.
- **Do not assume you will be turned away.** Some places cannot take a party
  of one at all — a twelve-seat chef's counter, a room built for groups — and
  some will, happily. Policies differ, and this post cannot tell you about a
  specific one. One short phone call answers it.

**At a fancier place, saying ‘table for one, and a seat at the bar or
counter if you have one’ — when you book, or when you arrive — does most of
the work: it turns an awkward walk-in into a request the staff can answer.**

Tipping, service charges and how bar ordering runs all vary by country and by
venue, and the way you learn them is the same as learning anything else about
a new city — slowly, and one visit at a time. That learning curve is covered
in [finding your regular spots in a new city](/blog/first-month-in-a-new-city-finding-your-regular-spots/).

## What to check before you sit down

Two minutes, before you leave the house:

1. **Is there bar or counter seating?** That is the seat where a solo dinner
   works, and most maps will not tell you. A phone call or the venue's own
   page settles it.
2. **Is it open now, and until when does the kitchen serve?** The bar staying
   open past the kitchen is common, and "open" on the sign can mean drinks
   only. The detail on what open now actually claims is
   [here](/blog/what-open-now-actually-means/).
3. **Does the menu work for one?** A menu built of sharing plates means your
   dinner is four plates at group prices; a place with small plates and
   single portions makes one person a normal order, not a special request.
4. **Is there a minimum spend or a time limit?** Some bars and counters cap
   how long you can keep a seat on busy nights, so ask if you plan a long
   meal.
5. **Do you need to book for one?** The better places book out solid, and a
   walk-in on a Friday is a coin flip.
6. **The practical bits: diet and access.** If you need a specific diet or
   step-free access, the same pre-check applies — check it before the walk,
   not after.

You can run all of this the old way — the phone call, standing on the street
corner reading the menu, asking at the bar — and that works fine. A map check
is faster for the open-now and the amenity items: NearEats, for instance,
shows whether a place is open right now, how close it is to closing, and
details like outdoor seating, so you can rule places out before you walk —
with the same honest limit as any map data, that the details can be out of
date. Whatever the check, the seat and how solo-friendly the room feels are
judged from the door.

The point of the list is not to plan the evening. It is to make the visit a
decision instead of a gamble.

## When solo is the wrong call, and when to leave

A few situations where sitting down alone is a bad fit, and the honest answer
is another night or another place:

- A set sharing menu where nothing works at one portion.
- A room that is loud by design, where your table is in the middle of it and
  your only conversation is with nobody.
- A venue that is full of parties, where a table for one reads as a mistake to
  the staff. If the vibe is wrong, the fix is a different visit, not a
  performance.

And when the visit starts wrong, the fix is usually one seat away: ask to move
to the bar or the counter, and a lot of rooms get quieter and easier the
moment you stop facing the entrance. Leaving before the meal is done is
allowed too; you do not owe the kitchen the rest of it.

One last note, plain and short: keep an eye on your drink and your tab, and if
you are in a city you do not know, telling someone where you are takes ten
seconds and costs nothing.

## The short version

- The awkwardness is mostly a seating problem: a seat facing the room feels
  exposed, a seat facing something does not.
- Counters, bars, communal tables, cafes and food-hall stalls are built for
  one person. Know the catch on each.
- At a fancy place, book for one, ask for the bar seat, and go at the quiet
  edge of service.
- Check before you sit: bar seating, kitchen close, a menu that works for one,
  minimums or time limits, and whether a booking is needed.
- If the visit starts wrong, move the seat or leave. You do not owe anyone the
  rest of the evening.
