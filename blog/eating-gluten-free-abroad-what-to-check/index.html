Eating gluten-free abroad: what to check before you sit down

Gluten-free travel fails less often than you'd expect. The places that fail you are usually predictable — here is how to spot them before the food arrives.

You have just landed, or you have just moved, and you know the first week of any new city is going to happen around a kitchen that does not speak your language. For most people that is a fun problem. For someone with coeliac disease or a wheat allergy, it is the kind of problem where a wrong guess has consequences measured in days, not minutes.

The good news is that most of the failure modes are predictable, and most of them can be eliminated before you order. What follows is a method that works whether you can read the menu or not.

The safe-by-construction list

Before you worry about the hard cases, build a shortlist of things that are gluten-free unless someone has gone out of their way to ruin them. These are your fallback orders in any restaurant, in any language:

  • Fresh grilled or roasted meat or fish, with vegetables or a plain side
  • Salads, with a check on the dressing and any croutons or cheese that might be pre-crumbed
  • Rice dishes where the rice is the thing — risotto is the one to question, because some versions get a butter-and-flour base
  • Soup that is clearly broth-based, with a question about thickening (see below)
  • Fruit, fresh vegetables, plain eggs, plain cheese

If you can eat one of these every night, the week is fine. The question is what to do when you want more than the fallback.

The words that mean gluten

You do not need to be fluent. You need a handful of words in the local language, and most of them are shorter than you expect. A few patterns that recur across languages:

  • The bread word almost always exists in some form: *bread, pain, pan, brot, chleba, xleba* and relatives. If a dish name contains it, that is your flag.
  • The pasta word: pasta, nudeln, makaroni and relatives. Same deal.
  • The word for flour or "with flour" is worth having, because batters and pan-coatings are described with it.
  • The word for "gluten" itself is increasingly on menus in tourist areas, and in smaller ones it is often the only gluten-related word present.
  • The word for "gluten-free" is on almost every menu that bothers to distinguish, and it is usually a shortened form you can spot visually even if you cannot pronounce it.

If you are in a country where the restaurant is likely to have a gluten-free option at all, the phrase "Is this gluten-free?" in the local language is worth writing down before you go. The waiter is a professional — they will know what you mean even if your grammar is a disaster.

The dishes that look safe and are not

This is where most bad experiences come from, so it is worth slowing down.

Anything breaded or battered. Cutlet, schnitzel, fish-and-chips, tempura, anything with a crispy coating. The coating is flour, and it is not going to be gluten-free unless it is a dedicated gluten-free fryer in a dedicated gluten-free section.

Anything pan-fried with a sauce. A flour-thickened pan sauce is the standard technique in a huge number of cuisines. "How is it cooked?" is the question here, not "is it gluten-free?"

Soup and stock. The cheapest thickener in the world is flour. A soup that is not thin is suspect until you ask. Broth-based soups that are clearly just meat, vegetable and stock are lower risk, but "clear" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Anything on toast. Poffertjes-style dishes, bruschetta, anything served on a bread base that you did not order separately.

Sauces you cannot see. A cream sauce might be fine and might be flour-thickened. A curry might be fine and might have a wheat-based thickener in it. The question is the same every time: "What is in the sauce?"

The pattern to internalise: if the gluten would be visible on the plate, you probably know it. If the gluten would be inside the cooking process, you need to ask.

What to ask before you sit down

If you have found a place that looks promising — one with a gluten-free section on the menu, a dedicated gluten-free kitchen, or a reputation for catering to it — do not skip the pre-visit question. A two-line message or a phone call is worth it:

  • "Do you have a dedicated gluten-free kitchen, or do you prepare gluten-free dishes in the same kitchen?"
  • "Do you have a separate fryer for gluten-free items?"
  • "Can you confirm that the [dish you want] is prepared without cross-contact with gluten?"

The second and third questions are the ones that separate a kitchen that has been told to be careful from a kitchen that has actually built a system. "Separate fryer" and "separate prep" are the answers that mean something. "We try our best" is an answer, but it is a weaker one, and you should know that before you commit.

What not to ask — and what to do instead

Do not ask "is this gluten-free?" about every single item on the menu. The waiter is not a food scientist, and the kitchen may not have a gluten-free spec for every dish. Ask about the specific dish you want to order, and about the kitchen's general approach, and accept that some things will be a no without an explanation.

If a place is genuinely unable to confirm that something is gluten-free, that is information. It is not a failure on their part — most kitchens simply have not built a gluten-free system — but it is a signal to move on rather than gamble.

The honest limit

No amount of checking makes a meal in a shared kitchen safe in the absolute sense. Cross-contamination is real, it is not always visible, and it is not always avoidable even in a careful kitchen. If you have coeliac disease or a severe allergy, the safest option is a venue with a dedicated gluten-free kitchen or a verifiable celiac menu, and even then you are relying on the venue's word about its own processes.

The method above is about reducing the odds, not eliminating them. That is the honest version of what any gluten-free travel advice can promise, and it is enough to make most weeks work.

The short version

  • Build a fallback list of safe-by-construction dishes and keep it in your head.
  • Learn five or six words: the bread word, the flour word, the gluten word, and the phrase "Is this gluten-free?"
  • Question anything breaded, anything with a sauce, and anything soup.
  • Ask the kitchen about its process before you sit down, and listen for "separate fryer" and "separate prep."
  • If a place cannot confirm, move on.
  • No check is a guarantee. Only the kitchen can speak to its own cross-contamination.

Checking first — whether that is a quick message to the restaurant, an app like NearEats that shows a venue's diet options at a glance before you walk over, or just the tourist-trap tells you can read on the street itself — saves the worst version of this: finding out at the plate. Walking in and reading the menu works too. The point is just to move the question earlier.

Common questions

Can I trust a restaurant that says it is gluten-free?

Trust the process, not the label. A dedicated gluten-free kitchen is one thing — a regular kitchen that has been told to avoid flour is another, and the second one depends entirely on how disciplined that day's service is. Ask what specifically the kitchen does: separate fryer, separate prep space, gluten-free flours kept apart. You do not need the answer to be perfect, but you need it to be real.

Which dishes are safe without asking?

The ones where the gluten would be obvious on the plate: fresh grilled or roasted meat or fish with vegetables, salads, raw or simply cooked things that have never touched flour. Anything breaded, battered, served on toast, or cooked in a pan that has had a flour-thickened sauce is a question, not a guess. Soups and stocks are the classic trap — flour is the cheapest thickener there is.

Is it worth looking for a place that caters to coeliac disease, or is any gluten-free option fine?

For a severe allergy or coeliac disease, yes, look specifically — and even then, treat the venue's claim as a starting point, not a guarantee. Cross-contamination is real and only the kitchen can speak to its own. If the stakes are a health emergency rather than an afternoon of discomfort, a dedicated gluten-free venue or one with a verifiable celiac menu is the safer bet.
