How to find good food in a city you don't know

You have landed, you are hungry, and every restaurant on the street looks equally plausible. Here is how to tell them apart in about ninety seconds.

You have landed, dropped your bag, and walked out of the hotel. It is somewhere between late lunch and early dinner, you are hungrier than you want to admit, and every restaurant on the street looks equally plausible. The one with the laminated photo menu is closest.

This is how most bad travel meals start — not through bad luck, but because being hungry in an unfamiliar place removes your ability to be picky at exactly the moment you need it most.

Here is a method that takes about ninety seconds and reliably beats guessing.

First, get out of the ring

Almost every station, cathedral, old town and major sight has a ring around it of about two hundred metres, inside which the restaurants are priced for people who will never come back. They are not scams. They are simply optimised for a customer who is there once.

The single most effective thing you can do is walk out of that ring. Five to ten minutes in any direction, and the prices drop, the menus shorten, and the proportion of people speaking the local language goes up.

You do not need to know a city to do this. You only need to know where the tourists are, and you already do — you are standing with them.

The tells that actually mean something

Once you are out, a few signals are worth more than the rest:

  • A short menu. A kitchen that does eight things does them because it can. A kitchen offering pizza, sushi, burgers and paella is running a freezer.
  • A menu in one language, with no photographs. Photo menus exist to sell to people who cannot read the words. That is a statement about the intended customer, not about the food.
  • Locals eating at local hours. If the room is full and nobody in it has a daypack, that is the strongest signal available to you.
  • Someone outside inviting you in. This is the reliable negative. Restaurants with a queue do not need a person on the pavement.
  • A handwritten or dated board. It suggests the menu changes, which suggests someone is buying ingredients rather than reheating them.

None of these are absolute. All of them together are rarely wrong.

Answer three questions before you walk

The tells above tell you whether a place is good. They do not tell you whether going there is a good idea right now. Three questions decide that, and all three are answerable before you move:

Is it actually open? Not "does it open on Thursdays" — is it serving now, and will it still be serving when you arrive? Kitchens close well before the venue does. A place that stops serving at 21:30 is not a dinner option at 21:20, ten minutes away.

How far is it really? Not the distance in metres. The walk. Cities with rivers, hills and one-way pedestrian systems make a "400 m" restaurant a fifteen-minute trip. Walking time is the number that matters.

Can they feed you specifically? If you are coeliac, halal, kosher, vegan, or you use a wheelchair, this is not an afterthought — it is the first question, and it is the one most likely to be answered by a shrug at the door after you have already walked there.

That last one is worth dwelling on. Most restaurant discovery is built around the assumption that any restaurant will do, and that finding food is about taste rather than access. For a lot of people it is not. Knowing that a place does gluten-free, or that it has a step at the entrance, is the difference between a meal and a wasted evening.

This is the gap NearEats was built to close: it shows the restaurants, cafes and bars around you along with whether they are open right now, what diets they handle, whether you can get in without steps, and how long the walk actually takes — before you commit to it.

When you genuinely cannot decide

Group decision-making about food is famously terrible. Four people will happily spend twenty-five minutes not choosing, by which point everyone is too hungry to enjoy whatever they eventually pick.

The fix is to remove the choice rather than improve it. Set a rule before you start — "first place we pass that is open and does vegetarian" — and follow it. The meal will be fine. The twenty-five minutes will not come back.

What to do when you get it wrong

You will sometimes eat badly. Everyone travelling does.

The useful habit is to note why, immediately. Too close to the station. Menu was six pages. Nobody local in the room. It arrived in four minutes, which means it was not cooked. Two or three of these and you stop making the same mistake, which is more than most people get out of a bad dinner.

And keep track of the good ones. The restaurant you loved on the second night is almost impossible to find again on the fifth, unless you wrote it down when you were still standing outside it.

The short version

Walk ten minutes out of the tourist ring. Look for a short menu, one language, no photographs, and locals eating. Before you go, check that it is open now, how long the walk really is, and whether they can actually feed you. If nobody can decide, use a rule instead of a discussion.

Ninety seconds of this beats an hour of wandering, and it is the difference between remembering a city for its food and remembering it for a disappointing plate of pasta near the station.

Common questions

How far should I be willing to walk for a meal?

Ten to fifteen minutes is usually the sweet spot. It is far enough to leave the immediate tourist ring around a station or a landmark, and close enough that you will not resent the walk if the place turns out to be ordinary. Check the walking time rather than the distance — a kilometre across a river or up a hill is not the same as a kilometre along a flat street.

Are online reviews useful when travelling?

Less than people think. Review counts in tourist areas are dominated by other tourists, which is exactly the crowd you are trying to escape, and a four-star average across thousands of reviews tells you almost nothing about tonight. Facts — opening hours, what kind of food it is, whether it can accommodate you — predict a decent meal better than an aggregate score does.

What is the single biggest mistake?

Walking somewhere without checking whether it is open. A place that closes its kitchen at nine is not open at ten, whatever the sign on the door says, and the disappointment of arriving five minutes too late is worse than the walk.
